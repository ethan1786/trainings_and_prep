# CCAT & SJT Assessment Prep

> Generic prep for cognitive aptitude tests and situational judgment tests.
> Used by: Coinbase, Datadog, and many companies via Criteria Corp, Crossover, SHL, etc.

---

## Part 1: CCAT (Criteria Cognitive Aptitude Test)

### Format

- 50 questions, 15 minutes
- You are NOT expected to finish — average score is ~24/50
- Target: 30+ (top 20%), 35+ (top 10%)
- No penalty for blanks — unanswered questions are not counted against you
- ~18 seconds per question

### Three Question Types

#### 1. Verbal Reasoning (~15-17 questions)

**What it tests:** Vocabulary, analogies, word relationships, sentence completion.

**Subtypes:**

**Analogies** — "A is to B as C is to ___"
```
BOOK is to READING as FORK is to ___
(a) drawing  (b) eating  (c) writing  (d) cooking

Answer: (b) eating
Logic: A book is the tool used for reading. A fork is the tool used for eating.
```

```
PHYSICIAN is to PATIENT as LAWYER is to ___
(a) judge  (b) court  (c) client  (d) law

Answer: (c) client
Logic: A physician serves a patient. A lawyer serves a client.
```

```
METICULOUS is to CARELESS as GENEROUS is to ___
(a) wealthy  (b) stingy  (c) kind  (d) abundant

Answer: (b) stingy
Logic: Antonym pairs. Meticulous is the opposite of careless. Generous is the opposite of stingy.
```

**Common analogy patterns to recognize:**
- Tool : Purpose (hammer : nailing)
- Worker : Client (doctor : patient)
- Part : Whole (wheel : car)
- Synonym pairs (happy : joyful)
- Antonym pairs (hot : cold)
- Degree (warm : hot, drizzle : downpour)
- Category : Member (fruit : apple)

**Sentence completion** — fill in the blank
```
Despite the team's ___, the project was delivered on time.
(a) efficiency  (b) setbacks  (c) funding  (d) support

Answer: (b) setbacks
Logic: "Despite" signals contrast — the blank should be something negative that was overcome.
```

**Odd one out** — which word doesn't belong?
```
Which does NOT belong?
(a) circle  (b) square  (c) triangle  (d) sphere

Answer: (d) sphere
Logic: All others are 2D shapes; sphere is 3D.
```

**Tips for verbal:**
- "Despite", "although", "however" signal contrast — the answer opposes the rest of the sentence
- For analogies, name the relationship out loud: "A is the tool for B" then find the same pattern
- If two answers seem right, pick the one with the SAME type of relationship, not just a related word
- Skip vocabulary words you don't know — don't spend 30s guessing

#### 2. Math & Logic (~18-20 questions)

**What it tests:** Arithmetic, word problems, number series, basic algebra, percentage/ratio reasoning.

**Number series** — find the pattern
```
2, 6, 18, 54, ___
(a) 108  (b) 162  (c) 72  (d) 216

Answer: (b) 162
Pattern: ×3 each time. 54 × 3 = 162.
```

```
3, 5, 9, 15, 23, ___
(a) 29  (b) 31  (c) 33  (d) 35

Answer: (c) 33
Pattern: differences are +2, +4, +6, +8, +10. So 23 + 10 = 33.
```

```
1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, ___
(a) 11  (b) 13  (c) 15  (d) 10

Answer: (b) 13
Pattern: Fibonacci — each number is the sum of the two before it. 5 + 8 = 13.
```

**Common series patterns:**
- Constant addition (+3, +3, +3)
- Constant multiplication (×2, ×2, ×2)
- Increasing differences (+1, +2, +3, +4)
- Alternating operations (+2, ×2, +2, ×2)
- Fibonacci-like (sum of previous two)
- Two interleaved series (odd positions and even positions follow different patterns)

**Word problems**
```
A store marks up items by 40%, then offers a 25% discount. 
If the original cost is $100, what does the customer pay?

Marked up: $100 × 1.4 = $140
Discounted: $140 × 0.75 = $105
Answer: $105
```

```
Train A leaves at 9am going 60mph. Train B leaves at 10am going 80mph 
from the same station in the same direction. When does B catch A?

At 10am, A is 60 miles ahead. B closes the gap at 80-60 = 20mph.
Time = 60/20 = 3 hours. B catches A at 1pm.
```

```
If 8 workers can build a wall in 6 days, how long for 12 workers?

Total work = 8 × 6 = 48 worker-days. 
Time = 48/12 = 4 days.
```

**Percentage shortcuts:**
- 10% of X = move decimal left: 10% of 350 = 35
- 5% = half of 10%: 5% of 350 = 17.5
- 25% = divide by 4: 25% of 240 = 60
- 33% ≈ divide by 3: 33% of 900 = 300
- Successive percentages: 20% up then 20% down ≠ original (it's 96%)

**Tips for math:**
- Don't calculate exact answers — estimate and eliminate. If the answer must be between 100 and 200, cross out 50 and 300 immediately
- For rate/work problems: find the total units of work, then divide
- For series: write the differences between consecutive numbers — the pattern is usually in the differences
- If stuck after 10 seconds, skip. Come back if time permits.

#### 3. Spatial Reasoning (~13-15 questions)

**What it tests:** Pattern recognition, shape rotation, sequence completion, mirror images.

**Pattern matrix** — 3×3 grid with a missing cell
```
Look at rows and columns independently:
- Row pattern: shape rotates 90° clockwise each cell
- Column pattern: shading alternates light/dark
- The missing cell must satisfy BOTH patterns
```

**Shape counting**
```
How many triangles are in this figure?
Strategy: count systematically — small triangles first, then medium (made of 2 small), 
then large (made of 4 small). Don't skip sizes.
```

**Rotation / unfolding**
```
Which figure shows the cube when unfolded?
Strategy: pick one face as anchor, check which faces are adjacent. 
Opposite faces in a cube can never share an edge when unfolded.
```

**Tips for spatial:**
- Trust your first instinct — your visual system is faster than conscious analysis
- For rotation questions: focus on one distinctive feature (a notch, a dot) and track where it goes
- For "which doesn't belong": look at symmetry, number of sides, shading, orientation
- For pattern matrices: check rows first, then columns, then diagonals
- These are the fastest to skip if stuck — move on and come back

### CCAT Strategy

**Before the test:**
- Take CriteriaCorp's free official practice test (one attempt): criteriacorp.com
- Do 2-3 timed practice sessions with any generic aptitude test (IQ test, SHL practice, etc.)
- Know your strongest category and plan to do those first

**During the test:**

| Time | Action |
|---|---|
| 0:00-0:30 | Scan the first few questions. Note which types appear first. |
| 0:00-7:30 | First pass — answer everything you can get in <15 seconds. Skip anything that needs extended thought. |
| 7:30-12:00 | Second pass — return to skipped questions. Spend up to 25 seconds each. |
| 12:00-15:00 | Third pass — guess on anything remaining. No penalty for wrong answers, so never leave blanks if time permits. |

**Pacing checkpoints:**
- At question 15: should be around 4:30 elapsed
- At question 30: should be around 9:00 elapsed
- If behind, skip more aggressively

**Category strategy:**
- Math people: do number series and word problems first (guaranteed points)
- Verbal people: do analogies and sentence completion first
- If spatial confuses you: do those last, guess if running low on time

## Part 2: SJT (Situational Judgment Test)

### Format

- 15-30 workplace scenarios
- Each scenario has 4-5 response options
- You either rank all options (most to least effective) or pick the best and worst
- No time pressure (typically 25-40 minutes for the full assessment)
- There IS a "right" answer — scored against an expert-validated key

### What It Measures

SJTs don't test knowledge — they test judgment and values alignment. Companies use them to filter for:
- **Collaboration** — Do you work with people or around them?
- **Communication** — Do you address issues directly or avoid conflict?
- **Ownership** — Do you take responsibility or defer?
- **Judgment** — Can you prioritize competing concerns?
- **Adaptability** — How do you handle ambiguity and change?

### Decision Framework

When reading a scenario, ask yourself: **"What would a mature, respected Staff engineer actually do on a normal Tuesday?"** Not the heroic answer. Not the political answer. The practical, professional one.

**The ranking hierarchy (most effective to least):**

| Rank | Action Type | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (best) | Address directly + collaborate | Talk to the person, offer help, solve together |
| 2 | Take ownership + inform | Fix the issue, then tell the right people |
| 3 | Escalate appropriately | Bring it to the manager when you've tried other options |
| 4 | Gather more information | Ask questions before acting (good if situation is ambiguous) |
| 5 | Do nothing / defer | "Wait and see", "it's not my job", ignore the problem |
| 6 (worst) | Blame / go around people | CC the manager without talking to the person first, public callout |

### Principles

**1. Direct > Indirect**
Talking to the person directly is almost always better than going to their manager, sending a passive-aggressive email, or hoping they figure it out.

**2. Action > Process**
Fixing the problem is better than scheduling a meeting about the problem. Creating a document about the problem is even worse.

**3. Collaborative > Solo**
"Let's figure this out together" beats "I'll handle it myself." Even when you CAN do it alone, involving the right people shows judgment.

**4. Assume good intent**
The coworker who broke something probably didn't do it on purpose. Approaches that start from "they made a mistake" are ranked higher than "they were careless."

**5. Ownership > Deferral**
"This isn't my responsibility" is almost always the worst answer. Even if technically true, the best answer is "I'll help, and I'll make sure the right person is aware."

**6. Proportional response**
Don't escalate a small issue to the VP. Don't ignore a critical production outage. Match the severity of your response to the severity of the situation.

### Sample Scenarios with Ranked Responses

#### Scenario 1: Code Quality Disagreement

> Your teammate pushes code that doesn't follow the team's coding standards. The code works correctly but is hard to read. What do you do?

| Rank | Response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (best) | Mention it in the code review with specific suggestions for improvement | Direct, constructive, proper channel |
| 2 | Talk to the teammate privately and discuss the readability concerns | Direct and collaborative, but PR review is the standard channel |
| 3 | Refactor the code yourself in a follow-up PR | Takes ownership but doesn't help the teammate grow |
| 4 | Bring it up in the next team meeting as a general standards reminder | Indirect — the teammate may not realize it's about their code |
| 5 (worst) | Let it go — the code works fine | Avoids the issue; technical debt accumulates |

**Principle applied:** Direct feedback in the right channel (code review) > private conversation > doing it yourself > indirect hint > ignoring it.

---

#### Scenario 2: Production Incident

> You notice a bug in production that's affecting a small percentage of users. Your team is in a sprint crunch and the bug isn't related to your current work. What do you do?

| Rank | Response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (best) | File a high-priority bug report, notify the on-call engineer, and offer to help if needed | Ownership + proper process + collaboration |
| 2 | Investigate the bug quickly to understand severity, then alert the team | Takes initiative, gathers info before escalating |
| 3 | Alert your manager and the team lead and let them decide the priority | Appropriate escalation but doesn't take personal ownership |
| 4 | Add it to the backlog and bring it up at the next standup | Too slow for a production issue affecting users |
| 5 (worst) | It's not your team's bug — focus on the sprint work | Abdication of ownership; users are being affected |

**Principle applied:** Production issues affecting users > sprint deadlines. Ownership means you don't wait for someone else to notice.

---

#### Scenario 3: Disagreement with Manager

> Your manager wants to ship a feature by Friday. You believe the current implementation has a security vulnerability that needs to be fixed first. What do you do?

| Rank | Response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (best) | Explain the specific vulnerability to your manager, propose a fix timeline, and suggest a compromise (e.g., ship with mitigation, fix fully next week) | Direct + solution-oriented + respects the deadline constraint |
| 2 | Document the vulnerability, share it with your manager, and recommend delaying the ship | Direct and responsible, but doesn't offer a compromise |
| 3 | Escalate to the security team and let them make the call | Appropriate for a genuine security issue, but bypasses your manager |
| 4 | Ship it on time and immediately file a ticket to fix the vulnerability next sprint | Respects the deadline but takes a risk on security |
| 5 (worst) | Refuse to ship until the vulnerability is fixed | Principled but unilateral — doesn't collaborate with your manager |

**Principle applied:** Raise the concern with data + offer solutions. Don't just say "no" — say "here's what I'd recommend instead." Going around your manager to the security team is valid but should be a last resort, not a first move.

---

#### Scenario 4: Underperforming Teammate

> A teammate consistently misses deadlines and their code requires extensive review feedback. Other team members have started avoiding working with them. What do you do?

| Rank | Response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (best) | Talk to the teammate privately — ask if they're struggling and offer to pair program or help them improve | Direct, empathetic, assumes good intent |
| 2 | Talk to your manager privately, sharing specific observations (not complaints) | Appropriate if you've tried helping directly and it hasn't worked |
| 3 | Offer to take on some of their work to keep the team on track | Helpful short-term, but doesn't address the root cause |
| 4 | Bring it up in a retro as a general team velocity concern | Too indirect — everyone will know who you mean, and it's embarrassing |
| 5 (worst) | Avoid working with them and focus on your own deliverables | Ignores the team problem; it will only get worse |

**Principle applied:** Help first, escalate second. Private > public. Specific observations > vague complaints. Address the person before addressing the manager.

---

#### Scenario 5: Conflicting Priorities

> Two different stakeholders have asked you to prioritize their requests. Both claim theirs is urgent. You can only work on one this week. What do you do?

| Rank | Response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (best) | Talk to both stakeholders to understand the business impact of each, then make a recommendation to your manager with your reasoning | Gathers info, involves the right decision-maker, shows judgment |
| 2 | Discuss with your manager and let them decide the priority | Appropriate escalation but doesn't show independent judgment |
| 3 | Pick the one with higher business impact based on what you know, and tell the other stakeholder when you'll get to theirs | Takes ownership but risks missing context |
| 4 | Try to do both by working extra hours | Unsustainable and doesn't address the prioritization problem |
| 5 (worst) | Tell both stakeholders that the other one has higher priority | Dishonest and will be discovered quickly |

**Principle applied:** When priorities conflict, gather context and recommend a decision — don't just punt it upward or make it alone without information.

---

#### Scenario 6: Inheriting Legacy Code

> You've been assigned to add a feature to a legacy codebase. The code is poorly documented, has no tests, and uses outdated patterns. What do you do?

| Rank | Response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (best) | Add tests for the area you're modifying, build the feature, and document what you learned — leave it better than you found it | Pragmatic: scope the improvement to what you touch |
| 2 | Spend time understanding the existing code, then build the feature following current standards as much as possible | Responsible but doesn't improve the codebase |
| 3 | Propose a larger refactoring effort to your manager before building the feature | Good long-term thinking but may delay the immediate need |
| 4 | Rewrite the relevant module from scratch to modern standards | Risky — rewrites often introduce new bugs and take longer than expected |
| 5 (worst) | Match the existing style to keep consistency, even though it's outdated | Consistency with bad patterns is still bad patterns |

**Principle applied:** Improve what you touch, don't boil the ocean. Ship the feature AND leave the codebase better. A full rewrite is seductive but usually not the right call.

### Common Traps

**The "heroic" answer:** "Stay late and do everything yourself." This sounds dedicated but is usually ranked lower than collaborative answers. Companies want team players, not martyrs.

**The "escalate first" answer:** Going to your manager or skip-level before talking to the person directly. This is almost never the best first move unless there's a power dynamic issue (harassment, safety).

**The "process" answer:** "Schedule a meeting to discuss." "Create a document outlining the issue." "Form a working group." These delay action and are ranked lower than direct resolution.

**The "technically correct" answer:** "It's not my job" or "that's the other team's responsibility." Technically true answers that show no ownership are always ranked low.

**The "both sides" answer:** "I'd consider both perspectives equally." This sounds diplomatic but shows no judgment. The test wants you to make a decision.

### Quick Reference: What Would a Staff Engineer Do?

| Situation | Good Response | Bad Response |
|---|---|---|
| Someone's code has a bug | Tell them directly, offer to help fix | Fix it silently / report to their manager |
| Deadline vs quality | Propose a scoped compromise with tradeoffs stated | Refuse to ship / ship garbage quietly |
| Two teams disagree | Facilitate a discussion, drive toward a decision | Pick a side without understanding both / avoid it |
| You made a mistake | Own it, fix it, share what you learned | Hide it / blame the tooling / blame the process |
| You don't know something | Say so, then go learn it | Pretend you know / deflect the question |
| Ambiguous requirements | Ask clarifying questions, then propose something | Wait for someone to tell you exactly what to do |
| Colleague is struggling | Offer help privately first | Complain about them to others / ignore it |
| You disagree with a decision | Voice your concern with reasoning, then commit | Silently sabotage / loudly refuse / passive-aggressive compliance |

## Quick Study Plan

| Day | Activity | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 | Take CriteriaCorp free CCAT practice test | 20 min |
| Day 1 | Review wrong answers — identify weakest category | 10 min |
| Day 2 | Practice 20 number series + 10 word problems (search "CCAT practice" on YouTube) | 30 min |
| Day 2 | Read through SJT scenarios above, internalize the ranking principles | 20 min |

| Day 4 | Final timed CCAT practice, focus on pacing | 15 min |
